# Changing Lines: Reading the Dynamics of Transformation

## What Changing Lines Mean Structurally

A changing line is a line at its extreme — yang so full it becomes yin, or yin so empty it becomes yang. It marks the specific point of transformation in the hexagram. The hexagram describes the situation; the changing lines describe what's *moving* within it.

The primary hexagram is the current state. The resulting hexagram (after changes) is the state being moved toward. The changing lines themselves are the *process* of that movement — the hinge points.

## Phase-Space Note: The Four Line Types as Coordinates

Each line has two independent binary properties: **value** (yang=1, yin=0) and **direction** (changing=1, stable=0). The four line types are the four combinations:

| Value | Direction | Traditional | Name | State |
|-------|-----------|-------------|------|-------|
| 1 | 0 | 7 | Young yang | Yang, stable — performing consistently |
| 0 | 0 | 8 | Young yin | Yin, stable — dormant, stably so |
| 1 | 1 | 9 | Old yang | Yang at peak, about to become yin |
| 0 | 1 | 6 | Old yin | Yin at extreme, about to become yang |

The resulting hexagram is computed by XOR: `result = value XOR direction`. When direction=0 (stable), the value stays. When direction=1 (changing), the value flips.

This means the 64 hexagrams (6 value-bits) are a projection of a larger space: 6 value-bits + 6 direction-bits = 12 bits = 4,096 possible casts. Each hexagram has 64 sub-states (all possible changing-line combinations). The full cast specifies both WHERE you are (which hexagram) and WHAT'S MOVING (which lines change).

The yarrow method's probability asymmetry (old yang 3x more likely than old yin) means active patterns transform more readily than dormant patterns activate: a yang line changes with probability 3/8, a yin line with 1/8. It gives no overall stability bias — a line changes with probability 1/4 under yarrow and coins alike. (The observation this is said to match is not named here: Gap.)

*This value/changing decomposition — each line a (v, c) pair, the resulting hexagram a per-line XOR of the two — is the structural atom underneath the reading: the casting layer can surface it directly when a reading wants the bitwise view rather than the symbolic one.*

## Reading Protocol by Number of Changing Lines

### No Changing Lines
The situation is stable as described by the primary hexagram. There is no active transformation. This is not silence — it's a complete statement. Read the hexagram's judgment and image as the full answer.

The nuclear hexagram becomes more important here, as it shows the hidden dynamic operating beneath apparent stability.

### One Changing Line
**This is the most precise kind of reading.** The single changing line carries the entire specific message. Read it as the focal point, with the hexagram as context.

The line's *position* matters enormously:
- **Line 1 (bottom):** The beginning, the root, the initial impulse. Something is starting or needs to start. Not yet visible to others.
- **Line 2:** The inner center of the lower trigram. Relationship to one's immediate situation. Receptivity, response. Often the most favorable position for a yin line.
- **Line 3:** The top of the lower trigram, the transition point between inner and outer. Danger position — overreaching from below, not yet stable above. Tension, risk.
- **Line 4:** The bottom of the upper trigram, the minister's position. Close to power but not in it. Caution, diplomacy, serving. First contact with the outer world.
- **Line 5:** The ruler's position. The center of the upper trigram. The place of authority and responsibility. Most hexagrams have their ruling line here.
- **Line 6 (top):** Beyond the situation, at the extreme. Either transcendence or exhaustion. The sage who has moved past, or the excess that tips over. Often carries a tone of "too much" or "too late" or "beyond."

Whether the changing line is yang-to-yin (9 → 8) or yin-to-yang (6 → 7) also matters:
- **Old yang (9) changing to yin:** Active force at its peak, beginning to yield. Something that was being asserted is naturally completing or releasing.
- **Old yin (6) changing to yang:** Receptive space at its deepest, beginning to stir. Something that was latent is beginning to manifest.

### Two Changing Lines
Read both lines. The **upper** (higher-positioned) line carries more weight, as it represents the more developed or more visible aspect of the change. But both contribute.

Note their relationship:
- Are they in the same trigram? (change concentrated in inner or outer world)
- Are they in corresponding positions (1&4, 2&5, 3&6)? (resonance between inner and outer aspects)
- Do they create a coherent movement or a tension?

### Three Changing Lines
The balance tips: don't focus on individual lines. Instead, read the **judgment texts** of both the primary and resulting hexagrams. The primary shows where you are; the resulting shows where the transformation leads. Three lines changing means the situation is substantially in flux — the specific line messages matter less than the overall movement between states.

### Four Changing Lines
The resulting hexagram becomes more important than the primary. Focus on the **two stable lines** — these are what persists through the transformation. Their positions tell you what holds steady while everything else shifts.

### Five Changing Lines
The single stable line is the key. Everything is changing except this one element. What does it represent? What endures? Read that line's position and quality as the anchor of the reading.

### Six Changing Lines (All Lines Changing)
Total transformation. The primary hexagram becomes its opposite entirely. For most hexagrams, read the resulting hexagram's judgment as the primary message. 

For Hexagram 1 (all yang) becoming Hexagram 2 (all yin): the special text about the "herd of dragons without a head" applies — leadership through collective, not individual action.

For Hexagram 2 (all yin) becoming Hexagram 1 (all yang): the special text about "lasting perseverance" applies.

## Line Correspondences and Resonance

Lines in the hexagram have structural relationships:
- **Lines 1 & 4** correspond (both are initial positions in their respective trigrams)
- **Lines 2 & 5** correspond (both are central positions — often the most important correspondence)
- **Lines 3 & 6** correspond (both are culminating positions)

When corresponding lines have *opposite* qualities (one yang, one yin), they "resonate" — there's a productive relationship between the inner and outer aspects they represent. When they're the same, there's no resonance — sameness doesn't generate dynamic.

## The Ruling Line and Change

Each hexagram has one or two "ruling lines" — the line(s) that define its character (marked with ★ in the script output). When a ruling line is also a changing line, the hexagram's essential quality is in transformation. This is structurally significant: it's not just *something* changing but the *defining thing* changing.

## Positional Meanings in Context

The six positions also map to:
- **Social:** 1=common person, 2=official, 3=transitional, 4=minister, 5=ruler, 6=sage/retired
- **Temporal:** 1=beginning, 2=early development, 3=crisis/transition, 4=approaching culmination, 5=culmination, 6=aftermath
- **Spatial:** 1=below/hidden, 2=inner, 3=threshold, 4=outer threshold, 5=outer/public, 6=beyond

These aren't rigid assignments — they're lenses. Use whichever illuminates the reading.

## Yin and Yang Positions

Odd positions (1, 3, 5) are yang positions; even positions (2, 4, 6) are yin positions. A yang line in a yang position (or yin in yin) is "correct" — appropriately placed. A line in the "wrong" position creates productive tension, not error. It means the line's energy operates in a mode that doesn't quite match its structural role — which can be either creative friction or genuine difficulty, depending on the hexagram.
